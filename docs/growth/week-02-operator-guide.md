A faster proposal generator will not help much if an inquiry spends most of its life waiting for someone to pick it up.

Before adding AI to a hotel group-sales workflow, follow a few inquiries from arrival to the next clear decision. Look for the handoff that stalls. Then choose the smallest change that removes that delay without weakening the approval process.

This guide uses an illustrative workflow, not a client case study. The timings and problems in your own operation need to come from your records.

## Follow one inquiry all the way through

Start with a recent group request. It might arrive through an email, a website form, a phone conversation, or an existing sales platform.

Write down five moments:

1. The inquiry arrived.
2. Someone accepted responsibility for it.
3. The team had enough information to prepare an answer.
4. The proposal was ready for review.
5. The reviewer approved it or requested a change.

Add a sixth if the approved proposal still waited before being sent.

Use elapsed time between these moments to find where the request waited. Separately record the minutes someone spent actively working on it. Those are different measures. Reducing typing time is useful, but it may have little effect on a two-day wait for ownership.

A small sample can reveal questions worth investigating. It does not establish a reliable average or prove that the same issue affects every inquiry.

## Separate missing information from missing ownership

Consider an illustrative request for a group stay. The sender provides dates and a rough room count but leaves out billing requirements and the decision deadline.

An assistant could prepare a request for the missing details. But that only helps if someone owns the next step. A draft sitting in another queue is still a queue.

For each stalled inquiry, ask:

- Was the next action clear?
- Did a named role own it?
- Did that person have the information and authority needed?
- Was there a visible route for an exception?
- Did anyone know when the request had been waiting too long?

Use anonymous case labels in the review. A shared workflow map rarely needs a guest name, contact information, or the contents of a private contract.

## Check the software you already have

Before building another system, inspect your current sales tools and their configuration. A shared queue, required fields, an assignment rule, a reusable email template, or a reminder may address the problem.

Compare that option with a custom integration using the same criteria: setup effort, reliability, staff training, data access, ongoing cost, and who will maintain it.

Do not assume a product supports a feature because a similar tool does. Check the current documentation and the actual plan your property uses. An existing feature that staff can operate may be a better first trial than a more flexible system that creates another place to check.

## Give automation a narrow job

A reasonable first experiment might prepare a structured inquiry summary and a draft request for missing information. It could surface the request in the existing review queue.

Define what the automation may do and where it must stop. For example:

- It may organize information already provided by the sender.
- It may flag missing fields.
- It may prepare a draft for review.
- It must not invent availability, rates, discounts, or contract terms.
- It must not send a commitment without the required approval.

The approval rule should match the property's actual operating policy. A human review step is useful only if the reviewer can see what was checked, what remains uncertain, and what action will happen after approval.

Also decide what happens when the automation cannot complete. A visible exception routed to an owner is a valid outcome. A silent failure is not.

## Measure the trial without promising savings

Run the smallest practical trial and agree on the measures before it starts. Track handling minutes, elapsed response time, missing-information loops, rework, and exceptions. Include the time spent reviewing drafts and maintaining the workflow.

As a simple illustration, saving five minutes on 30 inquiries releases 2.5 hours of weekly capacity. It does not automatically reduce payroll or create revenue. The value depends on what the team can do with that capacity and the cost of the new process.

Set a stop condition too. If staff cannot tell which inquiries have been handled, duplicate actions appear, or the review burden rises, pause and investigate before expanding the trial.
